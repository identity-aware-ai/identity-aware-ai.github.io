## 1. How Can the Law Protect Human Uniqueness in the Age of Self-Replicating AI?

**Authors:** Giorgio Pedrazzi

**Abstract:**

As artificial intelligence advances, systems are increasingly capable of simulating the expressive, behavioral, and even emotional traits that define human identity. This paper examines the growing tension between the technological capacity to replicate these traits and the legal frameworks that attempt to protect the uniqueness of the human self. We begin by outlining how language, tone, memory, affective expression, and other dimensions of personhood are no longer uniquely embodied or ephemeral, but instead digitally captured, measured, and encoded into machine-readable formats such as embedding vectors. In this new paradigm, the human persona is no longer inviolable—it becomes replicable. Drawing on comparative legal analysis between the European Union and the United States, we explore how current regimes—particularly the GDPR, the AI Act, and biometric privacy statutes—provide fragmented and often inadequate protection. While certain data types, such as biometric identifiers and medical information, are recognized as sensitive, more elusive traits like emotional style, moral tone, or cognitive patterns remain legally underdefined. The emergence of neurodata—data that reflects internal mental states, such as attention or emotion—in AI systems challenges traditional data protection laws by blurring the line between personal data and subjective experience. The legal vacuum becomes even more pronounced in cases of posthumous digital replication, where AI-driven "deadbots" or memorial avatars simulate the traits of deceased individuals without clear governance over consent, dignity, or narrative integrity. We argue that legal systems must evolve beyond a static, attribute-based model of identity and begin to address the narrative, affective, and cognitive dimensions of personhood. This paper proposes normative and technical pathways for extending protection to these underregulated facets of identity, calling for a shift toward a legal understanding of uniqueness that is not merely informational, but experiential and relational.

## 2. A Fair and Personalized Dementia Prediction Framework Using Longitudinal and Demographic Data from South Korea

**Authors:** Hong-Woo Chun, Lee-Nam Kwon, Hyeonho Shin, Sungwha Hong and Jae-Min Lee

**Abstract:**

This study addresses the critical issue of bias in dementia prediction models by developing a fair and personalized dementia prediction framework designed to account for demographic diversity in South Korea. Using data from the National Health Screening Cohort (NHIS-HEALS), which includes longitudinal data on 495,755 individuals aged 40 or older over 18 years, the research explores how existing AI models may produce inequitable performance across different demographic groups. The proposed framework integrates fairness-enhancing techniques with a personalized machine learning approach that incorporates individual demographic characteristics, such as age, gender, region, and socioeconomic status. Results show that the fair and personalized model maintains high predictive accuracy while significantly reducing demographic performance gaps, offering a more equitable solution for dementia screening in diverse populations.

## 3. From Perceived Effectiveness to Measured Impact: Identity-Aware Evaluation of Automated Counter-Stereotypes

**Authors:** Svetlana Kiritchenko, Anna Kerkhof, Isar Nejadgholi and Kathleen Fraser

**Abstract:**

There has been a growing interest in automated generation of counter-narratives for mitigating the harms of online stereotypical content. So far, the effectiveness of such interventions has primarily been assessed using qualitative methods, perceptions reported by content creators, and expectations of target audiences. Our study aims to measure the impact of counter-narratives on the actual audience. Focusing on stereotypes about women, we conduct a human evaluation (n=396) to assess the extent to which counter-narratives reduce stereotypical beliefs in content recipients as a function of their individual characteristics. We examine the impact of two automatically generated types of counter-narratives: corrections and amplifications. Our results show that both types of counter-narratives—on average—lead to small reductions in people's endorsement of stereotypes, but the effects vary greatly among individuals. Participants who initially endorsed a specific stereotype showed more attitude change after reading counter-narratives. This highlights the importance of carefully considering the characteristics of target audiences when deploying automated debiasing interventions.

## 4. MetaRAG: Metamorphic Testing for Hallucination Detection in RAG Systems

**Authors:** Channdeth Sok, David Luz and Yacine Haddam

**Abstract:**

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems enhance Large Language Models (LLMs) by incorporating external knowledge, yet remain susceptible to hallucinations—outputs that deviate from source material or contradict themselves. Traditional benchmarks for hallucination detection have been criticized for focusing narrowly on factual accuracy without considering the diversity of user populations and identities. We propose MetaRAG, a novel identity-aware framework that uses Metamorphic Testing (MT) to systematically detect hallucinations in RAG systems. Our approach generates diverse metamorphic relations—transformations applied to inputs that should produce predictable output variations—guided by demographic attributes such as age, gender, geography, and cultural context. Using the MetaRAG framework, we evaluate RAG systems across multiple datasets and models, exploring how variations in source documents reflecting user diversity affect model outputs. Our experimental results demonstrate that MetaRAG effectively uncovers subtle demographic-specific hallucination patterns that traditional methods miss, providing deeper insights into system robustness. By adopting an identity-aware approach to metamorphic testing, MetaRAG paves the way for more inclusive and reliable RAG systems.

## 5. Who are you, ChatGPT? Personality and Demographic Style in LLM-Generated Content

**Authors:** Dana Sotto and Ella Rabinovich

**Abstract:**

With the growing use of LLMs for a variety of use cases, from content generation to education, it is essential to understand the style-related characteristics of the produced text, both intrinsically and in comparison to human-authored content. In this work, we address the question of personality embedded in the texts generated by ChatGPT. We rely on a large-scale dataset of blogs which comes with a battery of personality assessments and demographic attributes of the authors, and prompt ChatGPT to generate posts that reflect human demographic and personality traits. We then use automatic models to assess the correspondence between personality and demographic profiles we aim to elicit from ChatGPT and those it actually generates. Our findings reveal that while ChatGPT largely mirrors the prompted personality and demographic traits, certain disparities emerge, illuminating areas of systematic bias in textual style. Furthermore, through testing consistency, we demonstrate that the attributes we extract from LLM-generated posts manifest themselves in a relatively consistent manner.

## 6. On the Interplay between Musical Preferences and Personality through the Lens of Language

**Authors:** Eliran Shem Tov and Ella Rabinovich

**Abstract:**

Musical taste has long been studied as a reflection of individual personality. In this work, we investigate the interplay between music and personality using computational linguistic analysis of Spotify reviews. We analyze a large-scale dataset of user-generated comments, employing both manual annotation to develop a comprehensive taxonomy of themes expressed in reviews and automatic methods to assess correlations between musical preferences and personality traits in language. We then explore the extent to which large language models succeed in reproducing language which is typical of distinct musical tastes. Our findings reveal nuanced relationships between personality and music-related language, with implications for music recommendation systems and digital humanities research on identity formation. By integrating computational methods with music psychology, we offer new perspectives on understanding how taste is manifested through digital communication and self-expression.

## 7. Political Bias in Large Language Models: A Case Study on the 2025 German Federal Election

**Authors:** Buket Kurtulus and Anna Kruspe

**Abstract:**

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly being used in political contexts, yet their potential biases remain a concern. This study investigates potential political biases in popular LLMs—ChatGPT-4, Google Gemini, and LLama 3.2—within the context of the 2025 German federal election. Using a combination of direct political questions and prompts that elicit party-specific responses, we assess whether these models exhibit patterns of political alignment. The results reveal that while all three models attempt to maintain neutrality, subtle yet statistically significant biases can emerge depending on how questions are framed. ChatGPT-4 showed the strongest tendency to align with certain party positions, while Gemini exhibited the most balanced responses. Furthermore, when asked to simulate the persona of specific political parties, all models demonstrated some capacity to shift their language and responses accordingly. This raises concerns about unintended influence in political communication and electoral contexts, highlighting the need for greater transparency in LLM training and deployment.

## 8. Identity by Design? Evaluating Gender Conditioning in LLM-Generated Agent Identity Profiles

**Authors:** Mattia Rampazzo, Saba Ghanbari Haez, Patrizio Bellan, Simone Magnolini, Leonardo Sanna and Mauro Dragoni

**Abstract:**

Recent advances in LLM-based agent frameworks have enabled the creation of synthetic personas for diverse applications, from conversational agents to virtual assistants. However, a critical question remains: do these models consistently represent gender in a balanced and non-stereotypical manner when creating agent identity profiles? In this paper, we present an evaluation framework to assess gender conditioning in LLM-generated agent profiles. We prompt state-of-the-art models (GPT-4, Claude, Gemini) to generate identity profiles for agents with specified gender attributes and analyze the resulting descriptions across occupation, personality traits, and interests. Our findings reveal systematic patterns of gender stereotyping: female agents are disproportionately associated with caregiving professions and emotional traits, while male agents are more frequently described in leadership roles and technical domains. These results have significant implications for the design of identity-aware AI systems and highlight the need for debiasing strategies when using LLMs to construct synthetic identities.

## 9. Testing LLMs' Sensitivity to Sociodemographics in Offensive Speech Detection

**Authors:** Lia Draetta, Soda Marem Lo, Samuele D'Avenia, Valerio Basile and Rossana Damiano

**Abstract:**

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) in content moderation tasks raises important questions about fairness and inclusivity. In particular, offensive speech detection systems must account for sociodemographic diversity in both speakers and targets. This paper investigates whether LLMs exhibit differential sensitivity to sociodemographic attributes when classifying offensive content. We construct a benchmark dataset containing offensive and non-offensive statements that vary across speaker identity dimensions—including gender, race, age, and sexual orientation. Using GPT-4, Claude, and LLaMa, we evaluate how these models rate offensiveness when identical content is attributed to speakers with different demographic profiles. Our findings reveal that LLMs demonstrate statistically significant biases, being more likely to classify content as offensive when attributed to certain demographic groups, particularly young speakers, racial minorities, and LGBTQ+ individuals. These biases persist across different models, highlighting systemic issues in how identity influences automated moderation. We conclude with recommendations for developing more equitable offensive speech detection systems that account for sociodemographic context.

## 10. IntersectionRE: Mitigating Intersectional Bias in Relation Extraction Through Coverage-Driven Augmentation

**Authors:** Amirhossein Layegh, Amir H. Payberah and Mihhail Matskin

**Abstract:**

Relation extraction (RE) models often exhibit biases related to demographic attributes such as gender, race, and ethnicity, which can result in disparate performance across different subgroups. Existing debiasing methods typically address single-axis biases but fail to capture the compounding effects of intersectional identities—individuals belonging to multiple marginalized groups. In this work, we introduce IntersectionRE, a framework designed to mitigate intersectional bias in relation extraction models through coverage-driven data augmentation. Our approach systematically identifies underrepresented intersectional subgroups in training data and generates synthetic examples to improve model coverage. We evaluate IntersectionRE on standard RE benchmarks, demonstrating significant improvements in fairness metrics while maintaining competitive task performance. Our findings underscore the importance of addressing intersectionality in NLP and provide a practical solution for building more equitable RE systems.

## 11. Identity-Aware Large Language Models require Cultural Reasoning

**Authors:** Alistair Plum, Anne-Marie Lutgen, Christoph Purschke and Achim Rettinger

**Abstract:**

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in multilingual and multicultural contexts, yet their ability to perform culturally grounded reasoning remains underexplored. This paper investigates whether current LLMs possess the capacity for cultural reasoning—the ability to understand and interpret language and concepts through the lens of specific cultural contexts. We argue that true identity-awareness in LLMs extends beyond demographic representation in training data to encompass nuanced understanding of cultural norms, values, and communication styles. To evaluate this, we introduce a novel benchmark dataset containing culturally situated scenarios that require models to reason about appropriate language use, social norms, and contextual meaning across diverse cultural settings. Testing state-of-the-art LLMs (GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, LLaMA), we find that while these models demonstrate surface-level cultural knowledge, they struggle with deeper cultural reasoning, particularly in non-Western contexts and in situations requiring implicit cultural understanding. Our results suggest that building genuinely identity-aware LLMs requires not just diverse data, but architectures and training approaches that enable cultural reasoning capabilities.

## 12. Neurodiversity Aware or Hyperaware AI? Visual Stereotypes of Autism Spectrum in Janus-Pro-7B, DALL-E, Stable Diffusion, SDXL, FLUX, and Midjourney

**Authors:** Maciej Wodziński, Marcin Rządeczka, Anastazja Szuła, Kacper Dudzic and Marcin Moskalewicz

**Abstract:**

The rise of generative AI models raises important questions about representation and bias, particularly concerning neurodivergent communities. This paper investigates how leading text-to-image models—Janus-Pro-7B, DALL-E, Stable Diffusion, SDXL, FLUX, and Midjourney—visually represent autism spectrum characteristics when prompted with identity-specific descriptions. We systematically analyze generated images across multiple dimensions: physical appearance, environmental settings, social contexts, and activities. Our findings reveal troubling patterns: all examined models consistently generate visual stereotypes that reduce autistic individuals to narrow, often infantilizing representations. Common stereotypical elements include: isolated figures, childlike representations regardless of specified age, puzzle piece imagery, muted color schemes, and restricted activity contexts. We distinguish between being "neurodiversity aware"—acknowledging and respecting neurological differences—and being "hyperaware"—fixating on stereotypical representations that reinforce stigma. Our analysis demonstrates that current models tend toward hyperawareness, potentially amplifying harmful stereotypes rather than promoting authentic representation. This work contributes to ongoing discussions about bias in generative AI and highlights the urgent need for more nuanced, respectful representations of neurodivergent identities in AI-generated content.

## 13. Trustworthy AI Through Dual-Role Reasoning: Ethical, Legal, and Psychological Internal Critique

**Authors:** Chengheng Li Chen, Antonio Lobo Santos, Marc Serramià Amorós and Maite López Sánchez

**Abstract:**

As AI systems become more autonomous and influential in decision-making, ensuring their trustworthiness has become paramount. Traditional approaches to AI ethics often rely on external auditing or post-hoc evaluation, which may be insufficient for catching problematic reasoning before deployment. This paper proposes a novel framework for trustworthy AI based on dual-role reasoning, in which an AI system performs internal critique across three dimensions: ethical, legal, and psychological. Drawing inspiration from dual-process theory in cognitive psychology and adversarial testing in machine learning, we develop a mechanism where the AI system simultaneously generates responses and evaluates them through specialized critique modules. These modules assess whether generated outputs align with ethical principles (e.g., fairness, transparency), comply with relevant legal frameworks (e.g., data protection, discrimination law), and consider psychological impacts on users (e.g., trust, emotional wellbeing). We implement this framework in a prototype system and evaluate its effectiveness across various scenarios involving identity-sensitive decisions. Results demonstrate that dual-role reasoning significantly improves the detection of potentially harmful outputs before they reach users, offering a promising approach for building inherently more trustworthy AI systems.
